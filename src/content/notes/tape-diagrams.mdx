# **Tape diagrams**

Tape diagrams are a **visual representation of quantities that use length to show equality**. Equal sides of an equation have the same lengths and are stacked on one another.

<div class="my-6 max-w-xs space-y-1 text-center font-mono text-sm">
  <div class="border border-[var(--border-color)] bg-[var(--bg-surface)] py-2 font-bold">x</div>
  <div class="border border-[var(--border-color)] bg-[var(--bg-surface)] py-2 font-bold">5</div>
</div>

For example, our equation "$x=5$" is pictured as a tape diagram above. It's important to understand that equations and tape diagrams are merely *different representations of the same thing* (they are "equal" in a sense). "$x=5$" is one way to say *"x is the same as 5,"* and so is our tape diagram.

For this tape diagram, it's arbitrary how we arranged it. We could have just as easily put the $5$ on top and the $x$ on the bottom, just as we could instead write the equation "$5=x$" to convey the same thing.

Tape diagrams are most useful to help visualize equations that use basic arithmetic ($+$, $-$, $\times$, $\div$). We will discuss extensions of this model for more complicated equations as we delve further.

### Negative signs

With negative numbers, it's fairly straightforward to represent them in a tape diagram by placing them on the other side of the diagram, so to speak. We can visualize subtracting from a quantity by *"cutting off"* that much length. This aligns with the algebraic idea of using **zero-pairs** to balance an equation.

However, with equations like "$3x = -18$" the visualization starts to break down. We can fix this by **relativizing our tape diagrams to 0**. Then, we anchor our variables to our constant quantity, which may lie on either side of 0.

Looking at the diagram to the left, it should be more visually obvious that each $x$ should account for taking us 6 to the left (i.e., **$x$ takes on a value of $-6$**).

The diagram to the right is a representation of "$-5x = 30$". Using a tape diagram can help us visually deduce what the value of $x$ must be, but just as in solving equations algebraically, we must be especially aware of potential sign errors and misconceptions. Note that each box above the "30" is "$-x$". So, while we can easily see that each box has a length of 6, we must be careful to see that **$x$ will equal $-6$, not $6$**.

As you can see, introducing negatives pushes tape diagrams to their limits, so to tackle harder equations, we'll want to build a *robust algebraic process*, which we'll get to shortly.

### **Tape diagrams for absolute value equations**

Extending our tape diagram model of equality to accommodate negative values gives us a nice visual way to solve **absolute value equations**.

So, how do we take something like $|x + 2| = 6$ and represent it visually?

First, what does this equation represent? It says that if I take the absolute value of some quantity ($x + 2$, in this case) it should equal $6$.

We've previously discussed how absolute value can be thought of as **<u>"distance from zero,"</u>** and that distance has no sign (it is *never negative*). So, if we relativize to $0$ and ask, *"where on the number line are we 6 away from 0?"* we end up with two answers (both at $6$ and $-6$). Let's visualize this:

### **Moving from tape diagrams to a robust algebraic process**

I like tape diagrams as a way to build a foundational, **reasoning-based approach** to solving for a variable or isolating a term in an equation. Look at the equations and their visual representation below, and try to reason what value $x$ should take.

When you look at the diagrams, hopefully you're able to discern that for (i), **$x$ should be 7**, and for (ii), **$x$ should be 6**. What did your reasoning look like?

#### Example reasoning:

Looking at (i), we can notice that there are **2 x's on the top and bottom that both account for the same length on each tape**. So, we can use the *leftover x* on the top and the rest of the diagram to reason what $x$ must be.

<div class="my-4 max-w-xs space-y-1 font-mono text-sm text-center">
  <div class="flex">
    <div class="border border-[var(--border-color)] py-2 w-7/12 font-bold">x</div>
    <div class="border border-l-0 border-[var(--border-color)] py-2 w-5/12">5</div>
  </div>
  <div class="flex">
    <div class="border border-[var(--border-color)] py-2 w-7/12 font-bold text-[var(--accent)]">7</div>
    <div class="border border-l-0 border-[var(--border-color)] py-2 w-5/12">5</div>
  </div>
</div>

Since there is a length of $5$ accounted for on the top tape to the right of $x$, and the total length must be $12$ to match the bottom, then $x$ must have a length of $7$ ($7+5=12$, making the two segments match in length). Therefore, the solution to (i) is **$[x = 7]$**.

Similarly, for (ii), we can see that each side (referring to the top and bottom tapes, respectively) has **3 x's accounting for part of its length**. That means that the *remaining segments must each have equal length*.

<div class="my-4 max-w-sm space-y-1 font-mono text-sm text-center">
  <div class="flex">
    <div class="border border-[var(--border-color)] py-2 w-1/4">x</div>
    <div class="border border-l-0 border-[var(--border-color)] py-2 w-1/4">x</div>
    <div class="border border-l-0 border-[var(--border-color)] py-2 w-1/4">x</div>
    <div class="border border-l-0 border-[var(--border-color)] py-2 w-1/4">10</div>
  </div>
  <div class="border border-[var(--border-color)] py-2 w-full font-bold">28</div>
</div>

Algebraically, this reduces our equation to $3x+10=28$. Think about what algebraic step would get us to that point from our original equation, and how that algebraic step relates to the reasoning we just performed by *"removing" 3 x's* from our tape diagram.

Our next step would be to determine what length the 3 remaining x's on the top must occupy. Since there is a segment of $10$ accompanying them, and the total must equal a length of $28$, we can deduce that the **3 x's must occupy a total length of 18**. 

<div class="my-4 max-w-sm space-y-1 font-mono text-sm text-center">
  <div class="flex">
    <div class="border border-[var(--border-color)] py-2 w-1/4">x</div>
    <div class="border border-l-0 border-[var(--border-color)] py-2 w-1/4">x</div>
    <div class="border border-l-0 border-[var(--border-color)] py-2 w-1/4">x</div>
    <div class="border border-l-0 border-[var(--border-color)] py-2 w-1/4">10</div>
  </div>
  <div class="flex">
    <div class="border border-[var(--border-color)] py-2 w-3/4 font-bold text-[var(--accent)]">18</div>
    <div class="border border-l-0 border-[var(--border-color)] py-2 w-1/4">10</div>
  </div>
</div>

Then, we ask ourselves the question, *"Can I divide 18 into 3 equal parts?"* and the answer is, of course, *"Yes!"* And we've reasoned that $x$ must be $6$.

Therefore, the solution to (ii) is **$[x = 6]$**.
